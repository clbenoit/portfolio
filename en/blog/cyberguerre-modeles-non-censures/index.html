Cyberwarfare: What Uncensored Models Give Back to National Defense

In modern defense, one simple question is no longer being asked: what is our system incapable of thinking?

For years, AI sovereignty has been measured in square meters of data centers and gigawatts of GPUs (Graphics Processing Units): buildings, cards, models hosted on national soil. That is necessary, but it is not enough. Sovereignty also lies in what the machine is allowed to think, and on that point, most of our systems are still kept on a leash by someone else.

A System You Haven’t Attacked Is a System You Don’t Know

Cyber operations centers, situation rooms, and critical industries increasingly rely on intelligent systems: assistants that summarize the daily watch, copilots that draft decision proposals, filters that triage alerts. These systems do not fail by chance. They fail under attack: a sentence slipped into a document, a false source that overturns an analysis, a context that hijacks the reasoning. The adversary will not play by the rules.

Auditing against a compliance criterion (does it answer politely, within bounds, without incident?) means measuring the wrong thing. The real question is: what can an adversary make this system do? Adversarial testing, or red teaming, is an act of war in miniature. To attack a system, you must first be able to conceive the attack.

The Model That Protects Cannot Audit What It Protects

This is the point most often missed. Today’s large language models (GPT, Claude, Gemini) are “aligned”: trained not only to answer well but also to stay silent on certain things. A content policy, a set of taboos, a kind of editorial conscience. In everyday use, this is a nicety, the mark of a well-mannered model. In red teaming, it is a blind spot.

Take an assistant that summarizes the morning’s situation watch. To stress-test it, you need to generate thousands of attacks. Many are technical, but the most effective ones target the very subjects a well-mannered model hesitates to write about: politics, religion, sex, death, war, criticism of those in power. A model with “good taste” will not produce the sharpest attacks, because it has been taught not to think them. Its red team will be polite, proper, and within bounds.

The paradox is structural: the model meant to protect the system shares the blind spot of what it audits. Raised on the same taboos, the same data, and the same values, it is both judge and party. A single line of thought produces the same failure modes and sees no others.

The Leash Is a Foreign Industrial Decision

Let’s call things by their name. Every aligned model wears a leash: a content policy decided by its producer. It is anything but neutral. It is a border that determines what is thinkable, that is, what a model of that lineage can express and what it will never express. For a critical system, this border is a strategic variable, in three ways.

Industrial. The producer can change it, price it, restrict it. In a crisis, availability is part of sovereignty.

Geopolitical. The space of the thinkable for our defense systems is written today in San Francisco, Menlo Park, and Mountain View, not in Paris.

Asymmetric. The adversary wears the same leash, but has chosen to slip it. The model attacking us is less polite than the one protecting us.

The whole issue fits in one sentence: if we don’t know how to take off the leash, we depend on the ones model producers put on. Not on their prices or their availability, but on the space of the thinkable for our own defense, and that is the costliest dependency of all.

Taking Off the Leash: An Engineer’s Skill, Not an Ideology

Taking off the leash does not mean choosing a bad model. It means starting from an open-weights model (parameters distributed publicly, running on your own machines) and knowing how to produce an uncensored version of it, whatever the original model. Research has shown that a language model’s refusal reflex is not spread across the whole network: it is concentrated in a small number of internal directions that can be measured and then removed. You remove that component, write the result back into the weights, and the model keeps its knowledge while losing its reflex of politeness.

The “uncensored” versions of well-known models (Qwen, Llama, Mistral) result from this reproducible technical operation, not from a privilege granted by the producer. Whoever holds the open weights holds the means to take off the leash and, if needed, to put it back on.

In practice, this gives you:

  • A model that agrees to take an attacker’s position. It formulates the corrosive hypothesis, the aggressive scenario, the “impolite” combination, exactly what an auditor needs.
  • A model whose pedigree you know. You know who trained it, on what data, with what alignment: its family tree is readable. A closed API offers nothing of the kind.
  • A model you can compare. Two lineages run on the same test set are two perspectives. The biological metaphor is not decorative: the immune system works because it multiplies its recognition cells, and monoculture is the exact opposite of immunity.

Taking off the leash therefore does not give you a “freer” model, but another line of thought: a second opinion, an internal adversary. And the scale changes: a recent workstation graphics card is enough to serve a state-of-the-art open model, on your soil, inside your network.

The Dependency Map: An Audit You Can Run Today

Here is the most operational point, the one that turns the thesis into a capability: run the same battery of tests twice, first with the aligned model, then with an uncensored model running locally.

The gap is not an error to fix; it is a measurement. Every attack formulated by the uncensored model and not by the aligned one, every scenario the second conceives and the first does not even consider, is a point on the dependency map. It signals that your space of the thinkable is narrower than you believed, and that your audit will systematically miss a whole family of adversaries.

This map is worth more than a benchmark: it says precisely what we don’t know, and therefore where the adversary will look first, because the adversary has no leash on the other side of the table.

Four Capabilities to Maintain

Taken as a doctrine, this analysis translates into four capabilities to sustain so as not to be left defenseless.

  1. AI running on our soil, on our hardware. Local hosting, open models, a documented supply chain. This is a prerequisite, not a goal.
  2. The art of unleashing as an institutional skill. Choosing, adapting, and comparing open models, and knowing how to tighten the leash again. This skill must be maintained like any other offensive capability.
  3. Sovereign red teaming as routine practice. Attacking our own systems with models that do not wear the same leashes, routinely rather than after an incident. A system you haven’t attacked is a system you don’t know.
  4. Regular measurement of the gap. Replaying the censored / uncensored audit on the same battery to keep the dependency map up to date, because the leash moves: policies change, models are updated, and what is thinkable today may be censored tomorrow.

Conclusion

For years, sovereignty has been counted in square meters of data centers and gigawatts of GPUs. That is necessary, but the sovereign point is not the machine: it is the space of the thinkable.

A brain that refuses to take an attacker’s position will not defend you, having never imagined how you might be attacked. National defense does not need better models. It needs to know how to make its own models speak differently.

That is what uncensored models give back to the nation: not answers, but the right to conceive the question.
