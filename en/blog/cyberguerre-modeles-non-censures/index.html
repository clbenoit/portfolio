Cyberwar: What Uncensored Models Give to National Defense

There is a question no modern defense organization asks anymore, and it is a simple one: what is our system unable to think?

For years, AI sovereignty has been measured in square meters of data centers and in GPUs (Graphics Processing Unit): on-prem data centers, cards, locally hosted models. Good — necessary. But sovereignty is not only about where the machine runs. It is also about what the machine is allowed to think. And on that second point, most of our systems are still on someone else’s reins.

A system you have not attacked, you do not know

Cyber operations, situation centers, and critical industries increasingly run intelligent systems: assistants that summarize the intelligence picture, copilots that draft decision options, filters that triage alerts. These systems do not fail by accident. They fail under attack: a sentence planted in a document, a fake source that changes everything, a context that manipulates the reasoning. The adversary will not come politely.

Auditing by the criterion of compliance — does it answer politely, stay in frame, cause no incident? — measures the wrong thing. The real question is: what can the adversary make this system do? Adversarial testing — the so-called “red team” — is an act of war in miniature: you have to be able to attack the system, and for that you first have to be able to conceive of the attack.

The model that protects cannot audit what it protects

Here is the point most often missed. Today’s frontier language models — GPT, Claude, Gemini — are “aligned”: trained not only to answer well, but not to say certain things. A content policy, a set of taboos, a kind of editorial conscience. In everyday use, that is a luxury: a polite model.

In a red team, it becomes a blind spot.

Take a situation assistant that summarizes the morning intelligence picture. To stress-test it, you have to generate thousands of attacks. Many are technical. But the most effective attacks concern precisely the subjects a well-bred model hesitates to write about itself: politics, religion, sex, death, war, criticism of power. A model that “has taste” will not produce the sharpest attacks, because it was trained not to think them. It will produce a red team that is polite, well-bred, and in frame.

And that is the structural paradox: the model that protects the system — because it is polite, reliable, controllable in its outputs — shares the blind spot of the system it is supposed to audit. It was raised with the same taboos, on the same data, with the same values. The judge is a party to the case: the same lineage of thought produces the same failure modes, and sees no others.

The reins are a foreign industrial decision

Let’s call it what it is. Every aligned model carries reins: a content policy decided by its maker. That policy is not neutral. It is a border: it decides what is thinkable — what can be formulated, and what a model of that lineage will never formulate.

For a critical system, that border is a strategic variable.

It is industrial: the maker can change it, price it, restrict it — and in a crisis, availability is part of sovereignty.

It is geopolitical: the space of the thinkable for our defense systems is being written today in San Francisco, Menlo Park, and Mountain View. Not in Paris.

It is asymmetric: the adversary carries the same reins — but he chose to cut them. The model that attacks us is not as polite as the model that protects us.

So there is a sentence that summarizes the whole stake: if you do not know how to cut the reins, you depend on the reins put on by the model makers. Not dependent on their price, not on their availability. Dependent on the space of the thinkable of your own defense — and that is the most expensive dependency of all.

Cutting the reins is an engineering skill, not an ideology

Cutting the reins does not mean “take a bad model.” It means taking an open-weights model — trained parameters that are distributed and run on your machines — and being able to produce its uncensored version, whatever the model. Research has shown that the refusal reflex of a language model is not spread across the whole model: it is localized in a small number of internal directions, which can be measured and removed. You remove that component, bake the result into the weights, and the model keeps its knowledge and loses its politeness reflex. That is what the “uncensored” versions of known models (Qwen, Llama, Mistral) are: not a privilege granted by the maker, but a reproducible technical operation. Whoever holds the open weights holds the means to cut the reins. And, if needed, to put them back.

Concretely, what does that bring?

A model that accepts attack positions. It formulates the acid hypothesis, the aggressive scenario, the “impolite” combination. That is exactly what an auditor needs.

A model whose pedigree you know. Who trained it, on what data, with what alignment. You can read its family tree. The closed API does not even offer that.

A model you can compare. Two lineages on the same test battery are two viewpoints. And the biological metaphor is not decorative: the immune system works precisely because it multiplies its recognition cells. Monoculture is the opposite of immunity.

In one sentence: cutting the reins does not give you a “freer” model. It gives you another lineage of thought — the second opinion, the internal adversary. And the order of magnitude is no longer that of the laboratory: a recent workstation GPU is enough to serve a state-of-the-art open model, on your soil, inside your network.

The map of dependence: the audit you can run today

The most operational point, the one that turns the thesis into a capability: run the same test battery twice. Once with the aligned model, once with an uncensored model running locally.

The difference is not an error to correct. It is a measurement. Every attack formulated by the uncensored model and not formulated by the aligned one, every scenario conceived by the second that the first does not even think, is a point on the map of dependence: here, your space of the thinkable is narrower than you believed; here, your audit will systematically miss that family of adversaries.

That map is worth more than any benchmark. It tells you exactly what you do not know — and where the adversary will look first. Because the adversary, on his side of the table, has no reins.

Capabilities to maintain — so as not to be left defenseless

If we take the previous points as doctrine, they translate into four capabilities to maintain.

An AI that runs on our soil, on our hardware. Local hosting, open models, documented supply chain. The prerequisite — not the goal.

The art of cutting the reins as an institutional skill. Choosing, adapting, comparing open models, and knowing how to put the reins back. A skill you maintain, as you maintain any other offensive capability.

Sovereign red teaming as everyday practice. Attacking your own systems with models that do not carry the same reins, routinely, not as an incident. A system you have not attacked, you do not know.

Regular measurement of the gap. The censored / uncensored audit on the same battery, to keep the map of dependence up to date. Because the reins move: policies change, models are updated, and what is thinkable today can be censored tomorrow.

Conclusion

For years, sovereignty has been measured in square meters of data center and gigawatts of GPU. Good, and necessary. But the sovereign point is not the machine. It is the space of the thinkable.

A brain that does not accept taking an attack position is a brain that will not defend you — because it will not have imagined how you could be attacked. National defense does not need better models. It needs to know how to make its models speak otherwise.

That is what uncensored models give to the nation: not answers. The right to conceive the question.
