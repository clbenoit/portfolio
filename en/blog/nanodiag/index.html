Real-time brain tumour classification via deep learning on Nanopore methylation data enriched by Adaptive Sampling

Context

For the past decade, the molecular classification of central nervous system tumours has relied on methylation profiles (Illumina 450K/EPIC arrays), interpreted via the reference classifier developed by the DKFZ (Heidelberg) [1]. This dependence on proprietary platforms and a centralised tool — whose recent shift to a pay-per-use model has worried many laboratories — is driving the search for open and sovereign alternatives.

Nanopore sequencing offers a promising path: tools like Sturgeon classify directly from native DNA methylation signals, with performance approaching that of historical approaches [3] — but they are trained on whole-genome sequencing (WGS). In clinical settings, however, sequencing the entire genome is costly and time-consuming: Adaptive Sampling, which retains in real time only molecules matching a targeted panel rather than reading the genome uniformly, makes the process faster and cheaper. The authors of Sturgeon show that Adaptive Sampling improves their classifier’s performance — yet they do not publish the panel used for this enrichment.

Hence our question: can we design this targeted enrichment panel ourselves, in a way that preserves diagnostic performance while integrating with the constraints of our shared platform?

This work also fits into a national context: according to the White Paper of the FFGH, 46 % of the French population may one day require a genetic test, and France must equip itself with sovereign, interoperable infrastructures [4].

Finding the right panel

Our initial panel, restricted to CpGs of genes known to be involved in brain tumours, covered only about 1.5 % of the genome — close to the usual Nanopore recommendation for maximising the number of samples per flow cell. That was insufficient: Sturgeon, trained on whole-genome sequencing, hallucinated on these too-sparse data. Although its authors show that Adaptive Sampling works,

First lesson: the classifier’s performance depends more on the breadth of the targeted genome than on the strict biological relevance of the chosen regions.

We therefore rebuilt the panel from the ~450,000 CpGs of the Illumina 450K arrays, widening the genomic windows around these regions to capture more informative long reads. After several iterations on window size, we obtained a panel covering nearly 30 % of the genome — twenty times more than the initial panel.

Restoring robust classification

On our validation samples, four approaches converged on the same conclusions: Sturgeon, NanoDX, PanNanoDX (its pan-cancer variant), and the Random Forest models, the state of the art for diagnosis in 2023 [5]. The Random Forest even reached 100 % confidence.

The biological information covered was sufficient to answer the clinical question: the Random Forest, the state-of-the-art tool in clinical practice, delivered the correct diagnosis with a maximal confidence score. The three deep learning classifiers also recovered the correct class, but their confidence scores were systematically degraded by the partial nature of the Adaptive Sampling data.

Outlook: fine-tuning the model

A natural path to regain the robustness still lacking in the deep learning classifiers would be to fine-tune the model on our own Adaptive Sampling data, rather than on whole-genome sequencing. This requires access to the model weights — which is not possible here: Sturgeon is distributed under a licence of the type “publicly accessible evaluation/research software + publicly downloadable weights”, but not open-weight in the permissive sense. The licence also explicitly designates Cyclomics as the contact for any commercial, clinical or diagnostic use. This is a structural limitation of the project, not merely a technical one.

Two additional requirements

CNV profiles. Classification alone is not enough: copy number variations (CNVs) are an essential layer of diagnostic information. Early versions of the panel created over- or under-represented regions; after optimisation, CNV profiles became comparable to those obtained with whole-genome data.

MGMT status. MGMT promoter methylation is an established predictive factor for treatment response in glioblastomas [2]. However, the regions exploited by reference tools (Rapid-CNS2, DKFZ) did not fully match those in our initial design, leading to discordant results. Conclusion: a panel optimised for classification is not automatically optimised for MGMT. A dedicated optimisation phase yielded a single panel that simultaneously satisfies classification, CNV and MGMT requirements.

Outlook: an LLM + RAG assistant in ROBIN

An improvement to this tool would be to add a conversational assistant to help biologists interpret results in a more intuitive and interactive way: explaining a classification, a CNV profile, an MGMT status, and suggesting the next diagnostic steps. To be reliable in this clinical context, such an assistant would need to be augmented with biological and literature knowledge via RAG. This would be a valuable feature for the Robin interface, serving as an interpretation layer on top of the raw results.

The most surprising benefit: real-time monitoring

One of the innovations that surprised me most about Robin was ultimately not biological but organisational. The classic workflow (sequencing → transfer → pipeline → interpretation) is sequential. With Robin, reads are analysed as soon as they are produced: classification, CNV profiles and quality indicators update continuously during the run.

This makes it possible to track diagnostic stability in real time, generate early alerts, and identify the point at which the accumulated information is sufficient — sometimes well before the theoretical end of the run. Sequencing can then be stopped, reducing flow cell wear and increasing the number of reuses and analyses possible.

In a shared-platform context, this is not merely a technical convenience: it is a structural lever for reducing turnaround times and making better use of equipment shared across multiple departments.

A regulatory friction

On the infrastructure side, the tools were deployed with a strict dev/prod separation, following DevOps best practices. But the ISO 15189 standard, designed for standard volumes, does not adapt well to massive sequencing — the absence of positive controls for ultra-rare diseases, combined with the pace of tool innovation, creates a real tension between regulatory requirements and diagnostic agility, as documented by the FFGH White Paper.

Conclusion

What began as a simple goal — reproducing Sturgeon’s performance with Adaptive Sampling — resulted in a complete pipeline combining molecular classification, CNV analysis, MGMT status, real-time interpretation and optimised sequencer usage. It shows that a local, open, and controlled alternative to centralised solutions is possible, while improving the operational efficiency of a molecular biology platform.

It also illustrates a challenge identified by the FFGH White Paper: France lacks medical bioinformaticians capable of designing these panels, integrating classifiers and industrialising pipelines. Without these hybrid profiles, at the interface of biology, computing and clinical practice, technological sovereignty remains a pious hope.

This work was carried out under the medical supervision of Dr Jean Boutonnat and in collaboration with Valentin Ripoche for the wet-lab component. I was in charge of the dry-lab side: panel design, integration and validation of existing tools and models, development of analysis pipelines, and production deployment of the solution in this clinical context.
